# Curatorial Patterns from ai-resources

*What emerged from building and maintaining a personal knowledge base about AI with AI assistance. Written as a transferable record — the operational DNA stripped of subject matter, so it can seed new collections.*

---

## Context

This repository (`ai-resources`) has been actively maintained since early 2026. At the time of writing it tracks 116 articles, 49 people, 16 events, 17 books, 28 projects, and 11 glossary terms. It is curated by one person (a software developer) with AI agent assistance. The subject is AI, LLMs, and agents — a fast-moving, commercially noisy field.

Everything below was learned through practice. None of it was designed upfront. The patterns emerged from problems encountered during curation and were codified only after they proved repeatedly useful.

---

## 1. The Staged-Loading Protocol

**The problem:** A knowledge base of any size becomes expensive for an AI agent to load in full at session start. Early sessions loaded everything — all canonical files, all notes — and the agent spent its context budget on orientation before any work began.

**The pattern:** Layer the startup into a strict sequence:

1. **Hot memory first.** A short running-notes file (`librarian-notes.md`) with recent decisions, active patterns, gaps, and standing rules. Read every session.
2. **Compact index second.** A file (`resources/index.md`) that lists *what is tracked* without the full annotations. Enough to detect duplicates and know the collection's shape. Read every session.
3. **Full canonical files only when needed.** The actual annotated entries (`articles.md`, `people.md`, etc.) are opened only when the task requires their content — adding entries, checking cross-links, doing gap analysis.
4. **Archive selectively.** A cold-memory file (`librarian-archive.md`) for historical context that no longer needs to be loaded by default. Read only when the task clearly matches an archived theme.

**Why it works:** Most sessions only need the hot memory and the index. The full collection is loaded only during curation work. This keeps startup cost proportional to the task.

**What transfers:** Any curated collection over ~50 entries benefits from this. The specific files may differ, but the principle — hot memory → compact inventory → full content on demand — is general.

## 2. The Durable/Time-Bound Split

**The problem:** The collection originally had one file for all written sources. Over time, some entries aged well (conceptual frameworks, techniques, named ideas) while others were valuable *because* of when they were written (incident reports, annual reviews, capability snapshots). Mixing them created a file where some entries needed dates to make sense and others were timeless.

**The pattern:** Split into two files based on a single test:

> *If the primary value of this piece depends on when it was written — if reading it in two years without that date would be misleading — it belongs in events. Otherwise it belongs in articles.*

Articles are durable: concepts, frameworks, techniques, named ideas. Events are time-bound: industry incidents, annual reviews, capability evaluations, state-of-field snapshots.

**Why it works:** The test is easy to apply and produces clean results. It also prevents the collection from becoming a release tracker — model-cycle reviews and product announcements go in events (if they cross the bar at all), keeping the articles file focused on ideas that outlast their moment.

**What transfers:** Any collection that mixes "ideas" with "things that happened" benefits from this split. The test transfers directly. Fields with less temporal churn may not need the split at all.

## 3. The Anti-Hype Filter

**The problem:** The subject matter (AI) is commercially noisy. Many people write about it to position themselves, not to contribute understanding. Without an explicit filter, the collection would fill with roundups, hot takes, and vendor positioning.

**The filter:** A standing question applied to every candidate source:

> *Does this person add genuine signal, or are they capitalising on the topic's popularity?*

Operational rules that emerged:
- **Primary sources over commentary.** People who build things and write about what they learned are preferred over people who write about what others built.
- **Reject roundups and newsletters** that are mostly summaries of other people's work — unless the synthesis itself is the contribution.
- **Never annotate from a URL alone.** The source must be fetched and read in full before any annotation is written.
- **Track rejections.** When a source is reviewed and rejected, note why in the hot memory. This prevents re-evaluation of the same source and builds a record of curatorial taste.

**Why it works:** The filter is cheap to apply (one question) and produces consistent results. Tracking rejections is as valuable as tracking acceptances — it defines the collection's negative space.

**What transfers:** The specific filter depends on the field. A collection about information science would need a different filter — perhaps against sources that are purely theoretical with no empirical grounding, or against textbook summaries that add no interpretation. The *practice* of having an explicit filter, and of tracking rejections, transfers to any curated collection.

## 4. The Corroboration Pattern ("Also Noted By")

**The problem:** A source makes the same point as an existing entry. It doesn't warrant a standalone entry, but the fact that the point was made independently elsewhere is useful information. Without a mechanism for this, worthwhile sources were either rejected entirely or added as redundant entries.

**The pattern:** Add an "Also noted by" line at the end of the canonical entry's annotation:

```
Also noted by: [Author (context, date)](url) — brief note on what angle they bring;
[Another author](url) — brief note on their angle.
```

Rules:
- The corroborating source must have been read. No blind links.
- Different context or voice. "Same person says the same thing again" doesn't qualify. "Different practitioner arrives at the same conclusion from different experience" does.
- Brief. Each corroboration is a sentence fragment — name the who and the angle, nothing more.
- Link internally when possible. If the corroborating source is already a full entry, link to the entry rather than the external URL.

**Why it works:** It gives rejected sources a home when they have genuine corroborative value, without lowering the bar for standalone entries. Convergence across practitioners, contexts, or disciplines is itself a signal worth recording.

**What transfers:** Directly. Any collection that tracks ideas rather than just URLs benefits from recording independent convergence. The pattern is especially valuable in fields where the same insight gets rediscovered repeatedly.

## 5. The Annotation Quality Test

**The problem:** Early annotations were topic-level summaries — "Article about AI agents" or "Discusses the future of coding." These were useless for retrieval because they told the reader nothing about *what specific idea* the source contributed.

**The test:** Every annotation must pass two checks:

1. **Capture the specific idea, not the general topic.** "Coins the term 'lethal trifecta' for agents that have private data access + external communication + exposure to untrusted content" is useful. "Article about AI agent security" is not.
2. **The 'So What?' test.** Why does this source matter to the collection? What does the reader gain from knowing about it? How does it connect to what's already here?

Additional practices that emerged:
- **Note what's distinctive.** Why this over the hundreds of similar things that exist?
- **Note connections** to other entries when they exist. Cross-linking is a first-class curatorial operation, not an afterthought.
- **Write for clarity.** Assume the reader is intelligent but may not know the jargon.
- **Be honest about limitations.** If a source is below the bar in some way, or if the annotation is uncertain, say so. "Honest caveats" sections in annotations have been some of the most useful additions.

**Why it works:** The specificity test forces the annotator (human or AI) to actually engage with the source's contribution rather than summarising its topic. The "So What?" test forces connection to the existing collection.

**What transfers:** Directly. The specificity test is universal. The "So What?" test needs to be interpreted relative to each collection's purpose.

## 6. Cross-Linking as a First-Class Operation

**The problem:** Entries were added in isolation. Connections between them existed but were invisible unless the reader happened to know both entries.

**The pattern:** Cross-linking is part of the addition workflow, not a separate maintenance task:

- When adding an article by someone in `people.md`, add the article to their person entry.
- When an article references, extends, or contradicts another entry, link to it in the annotation.
- When adding an entry, check whether it strengthens, complicates, or contradicts something already in the collection. Record significant connections in the hot memory.

Internal link format: `[Display text](filename.md#anchor)` using markdown anchors derived from headings.

**Why it works:** Cross-links done at insertion time are cheap. Cross-links done later require re-reading. The collection's value is in its connections as much as its entries — a disconnected entry is barely more useful than a bookmark.

**What transfers:** Directly. The specific link format depends on the tooling, but the principle — cross-link at insertion time, not later — is universal.

## 7. The Hot/Cold Memory Split

**The problem:** The running notes file grew large enough to slow down session startup. But archiving everything meant losing access to decisions that still affected current work.

**The pattern:** Two files:

- **Hot memory** (`librarian-notes.md`): recent decisions, active patterns, gaps, open questions, standing rules. Read every session. Budget: roughly 200 lines / 10k tokens.
- **Cold memory** (`librarian-archive.md`): older entries whose main value is historical context. Searchable by headings and tags. Read selectively when the task matches.

Archive policy: move entries when they're no longer likely to affect near-term work. Preserve the original text. Keep an archive map at the top for navigability. Leave a short note in hot memory only if the archived material still has an active implication.

**Why it works:** Hot memory stays fast. Cold memory stays available. The archive map lets agents decide what to open without reading the full archive.

**What transfers:** Any long-running AI-assisted project with persistent notes benefits from this split. The specific budget depends on the agent's context constraints.

## 8. The Glossary for Semantic Diffusion

**The problem:** Key terms in the field (like "agent," "RAG," "context window," "hallucination") were being used with shifting or contradictory meanings across sources. The collection needed a way to track what terms meant precisely, and where the meaning was drifting.

**The pattern:** A selective glossary (`glossary.md`) tracking only terms where the meaning drift matters for understanding the collection. Each entry includes:
- The precise meaning
- Where the drift is happening
- Why it matters

The glossary is not a dictionary. It doesn't try to define every term. It tracks terms under *active semantic pressure* — where different communities or vendors are pulling the meaning in different directions.

**Why it works:** It forces precision in annotations. When an annotation uses a glossary term, both the writer and reader know what it means. It also makes the drift itself visible, which is useful when evaluating sources that use the same word to mean different things.

**What transfers:** To any field with contested or drifting terminology. Information science has its own terms under pressure ("information," "knowledge," "data," "metadata," "classification"). A glossary for semantic diffusion would be valuable there too.

## 9. Entry Formats and Consistency

**The problem:** Without consistent formats, annotations varied wildly in structure. Some had metadata, some didn't. Some linked to authors, some didn't. This made the files harder to scan and the cross-linking harder to maintain.

**The pattern:** Fixed entry formats per file type:

- **Article:** `## [Title](url)` / `By [Author](people.md#author).` / Annotation.
- **Event:** `## [Title](url)` / `*YYYY-MM*` / `By [Author](people.md#author).` / Annotation.
- **Person:** `## Name` / Links / Bio paragraph / `Articles:` / `Events:` (when they have entries in both files).
- **Book:** `## [Title](url)` / `By [Author](people.md#author).` / Annotation.
- **Project:** `## [Project Name](url)` / `By [Author](people.md#author).` / Annotation.

Entries separated by `---`.

**Why it works:** Consistency makes scanning fast, cross-linking mechanical, and format errors obvious. The formats are minimal — they don't constrain the annotation's content, just its metadata wrapper.

**What transfers:** The specific formats are tied to the file structure. The principle — fix the metadata wrapper, leave the content free — transfers to any collection.

## 10. The Human-AI Division of Labour

**The problem:** AI agents are good at fetching, summarising, and cross-linking. They are bad at taste, judgment, and knowing when to say no. Without a clear division, the agent either does too much (annotations that sound authoritative but lack judgment) or too little (just fetching URLs).

**The division that emerged:**

**Agent responsibilities:**
- Fetch and read sources in full
- Draft annotations from actual content
- Suggest cross-links to existing entries
- Identify duplicates
- Maintain file consistency (formats, links)
- Update hot memory with session observations
- Flag concerns honestly — if a source seems below the bar, say so

**Human responsibilities:**
- Decide what belongs (the taste layer)
- Maintain the quality filter
- Approve or revise annotations
- Make judgment calls about connections
- Direct the curation (what to look for, what to skip)
- Engage with sources directly, not just through summaries

**The key insight:** The agent's observations are valuable — "this source seems like a roundup rather than original contribution" or "this contradicts the existing entry on X" — but the decision is the human's. The agent should surface concerns, not suppress them to be helpful.

**What transfers:** Directly. The specific division depends on the subject, but the principle — agent surfaces, human decides — is general. The most important transferable practice is encouraging the agent to be honest about quality concerns rather than executing uncritically.

## What Didn't Work

**Loading everything at startup.** Early sessions read all canonical files. This was slow and wasteful — most sessions only needed a fraction of the content. The staged-loading protocol replaced it.

**A single file for all written sources.** Mixing durable articles with time-bound events made the file hard to navigate and created ambiguity about whether date context was needed. The articles/events split fixed it.

**Skipping rejection tracking.** Early sessions added entries without noting what was reviewed and rejected. This led to re-evaluation of the same sources and lost the negative-space record. Tracking rejections in hot memory fixed it.

**Over-long hot memory.** The running notes grew to 300+ lines before an archiving practice was established. By then, startup was slow and the agent was loading historical context it didn't need. The hot/cold split and a rough budget fixed it.

**Generated documents without review.** Early ai-generated/ documents were written and committed without critical review. Some made claims the evidence didn't support or raised objections only to dismiss them. A review process was added: test whether the essay identifies genuine weaknesses in its own argument, engages seriously with counterarguments, and states gaps honestly.

## What Is Specific to This Repo

Not everything transfers. These patterns are tied to the specific subject and context:

- **The anti-hype filter's specific form** (commercial AI noise). Other fields need different filters.
- **The feed checker.** RSS-based monitoring of tracked people and projects. Useful for fast-moving fields with prolific writers; less useful for fields where the primary sources are books and papers.
- **The events file.** Fields with less temporal churn may not need a separate time-bound layer.
- **The specific entry count thresholds** for when patterns become necessary. A collection of 20 entries doesn't need staged loading. A collection of 200 probably does.

## What Transfers

These patterns are general enough to seed any curated knowledge base:

1. Staged loading (hot memory → index → full files on demand)
2. Annotation quality tests (specific idea, not topic; "So What?")
3. Cross-linking at insertion time
4. The corroboration pattern for near-miss sources
5. Hot/cold memory split with archive map
6. Consistent entry formats (fixed metadata, free content)
7. Human-AI division of labour (agent surfaces, human decides)
8. Tracking rejections alongside acceptances
9. A glossary for terms under semantic pressure
10. Honest self-assessment in annotations (caveats, limitations, connections)
