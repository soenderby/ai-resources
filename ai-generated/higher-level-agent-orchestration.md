# The Loop Above the Loop: Higher-Level Orchestration and the Limits of the Outer Loop

Every coding agent is already a loop. Strip away the branding and an agent is [a model, a system prompt, and some tools running in a cycle](https://simonwillison.net/guides/agentic-engineering-patterns/how-coding-agents-work/): the model looks at the situation, calls a tool, reads the result, and decides whether to go again. Armin Ronacher calls this the **inner loop** — [the model calling tools until it decides it is done](https://lucumr.pocoo.org/2026/6/23/the-coming-loop/). The recent wave of writing about "higher-level orchestration" is, almost without exception, about wrapping *that* loop in progressively higher loops: a loop that decides the inner loop stopped too early, a loop that steers several inner loops at once, a loop that rebuilds the environment the inner loops run in, and — at the top — a loop that improves the improvement process itself.

The field has been assembling this stack one blog post at a time, usually without naming it as a single structure. This essay names it. The rungs are real and worth distinguishing. But the central argument here is a deflationary one: **adding an outer loop does not create autonomy. It relocates the point where human judgment is required — and every rung is load-bearing only to the exact degree that it has a trustworthy signal to close on.** The higher you climb, the more the whole edifice rests on one unglamorous thing: a verifier good enough to tell the loop when it is wrong. Most of the interesting failures, and most of the interesting engineering, are about that verifier and not about the loop.

## The ladder

It helps to lay the rungs out explicitly, because the discourse tends to collapse them.

**Rung 0 — the inner loop.** The agent's own perceive–act–observe cycle. This is the thing every other loop wraps. It terminates when the model decides it is finished, which is exactly the property the higher loops exist to override.

**Rung 1 — the naive outer loop (the Ralph loop).** Geoffrey Huntley's Ralph technique is the purest form: [`while :; do cat PROMPT.md | claude-code; done`](https://ghuntley.com/ralph/). Feed the same prompt to a fresh agent, over and over, and let a durable artifact (a spec, a progress file, a git history) carry state between iterations. RepoMirror's ["We Put a Coding Agent in a While Loop and It Shipped 6 Repos Overnight"](https://github.com/repomirrorhq/repomirror/blob/main/repomirror.md) is the canonical field report. The Ralph loop's whole promise is that it appears to remove the human from the cycle.

**Rung 2 — the middle loop.** Annie Vella's [research-backed naming](https://annievella.com/posts/the-middle-loop/) of the supervisory work that sits between the inner loop (coding) and the outer loop (CI/CD, deploy, monitor): **directing, evaluating, correcting**. Her longitudinal study of 158 engineers found a statistically significant shift from creation work toward verification work — and, crucially, that the time freed from coding did *not* flow upstream into design. It flowed into a new, unoptimized category of steering the machine. The middle loop is where the human actually lives once the agent is doing the typing.

**Rung 3 — the harness loop.** This is Ronacher's **outer loop**: an external system that judges whether the work is actually finished and pushes the agent past where it would have stopped on its own. Anthropic's ["Effective harnesses for long-running agents"](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents) gives the pattern operational detail — an initializer agent, a progress file, one feature per session, JSON task lists the model won't casually edit. OpenAI's Ryan Lopopolo names [**harness engineering**](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering) as a discipline after shipping ~1M lines of agent-written code: the human work becomes "designing environments, feedback loops, and control systems," and the recurring fix was never "try harder" but "what capability is missing?" Birgitta Böckeler's ["Harness engineering for coding agent users"](https://martinfowler.com/articles/harness-engineering.html) (April 2026) adds a control-theory frame that will matter later: she splits the harness into **guides** (feedforward — steer before generation) and **sensors** (feedback — enable self-correction after), and splits both into *computational* (deterministic: linters, tests) versus *inferential* (AI judgment).

**Rung 4 — loop engineering.** In June 2026 Addy Osmani gave the top of the practical stack a name and an anatomy. ["Loop Engineering"](https://addyosmani.com/blog/loop-engineering/) sits, in his words, "one floor above the harness": where harness engineering designs the environment *one agent* runs in, loop engineering designs the system that "runs on timers, spawns helper agents, verifies, and sustains itself across iterations while the human is absent." Its building blocks are automations, worktrees, skills, connectors (MCP), and subagents, plus persistent external state because models forget between runs. The catalyst was a Peter Steinberger line — ["stop prompting agents; design loops that prompt your agents"](https://x.com/steipete/status/2063697162748260627) — echoed almost simultaneously by Boris Cherny, who built Claude Code: *"I don't prompt Claude anymore. I have loops running. They're the ones prompting Claude."* Two well-placed practitioners echoing a slogan in the same week is a signal that the *practice* has spread among power users — not, on its own, proof that a distinct engineering discipline exists. Take it as evidence of where attention is moving, and judge the discipline on its substance below.

**Rung 5 — the self-improving loop.** The top rung folds the ladder back on itself: a loop that edits the harness. Lilian Weng's ["Harness Engineering for Self-Improvement"](https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2026-07-04-harness/) collects the vocabulary — Agentic Context Engineering, Meta-Harness (harness code as a search space), Self-Harness (agents improving their own scaffolding through bounded self-proposals). The academic version has arrived too: **Agentic Harness Engineering** ([Lin et al., arXiv 2604.25850](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.25850)) makes the harness an observability-driven, self-rewriting object and reports Terminal-Bench 2 gains from 69.7% to 77.0%. Huntley's [Loom](https://github.com/ghuntley/loom) pushes the Ralph lineage toward "evolutionary software" that self-heals and, in his framing, eventually optimizes products for revenue.

That is the stack: inner loop → Ralph → middle loop → harness loop → loop engineering → self-harness. Each rung buys leverage. The question is what it pays for that leverage with.

A caution about the ladder before leaning on it: it is an idealization, and real systems blur it. Rung 2 is the odd one out — Vella's middle loop is a description of *human supervisory labor*, not a machine loop that encloses the inner loop, so it sits orthogonal to the others rather than neatly between rungs 1 and 3. The word "outer loop" is itself overloaded: Vella uses it for CI/CD/deploy/monitor, while Ronacher and this essay use it for the harness. And it is a fair question whether Rung 4 ("loop engineering") is a genuinely new floor or a rebranding — Osmani's own description of it (timers, helper agents, verification, persistence across iterations) is largely the Ralph loop of Rung 1 running inside the harness of Rung 3, and the evidence that it is a distinct discipline is mostly his "one floor above" framing plus a pair of practitioners echoing a catchy slogan. The ladder is useful for seeing the progression; it should not be mistaken for a clean taxonomy.

## The loop closes only on its signal

Here is the deflation. A Ralph loop looks like it removes the human, but it does no such thing. It silently *assumes* a verifier — a test suite, a spec precise enough to check against, a reviewer, a benchmark — and the loop is only as good as that oracle. Where the oracle is absent, the loop produces confident garbage at machine speed and calls it done.

The strongest objection to this comes from a place the essay must not dodge: self-play. AlphaZero and AlphaProof close a loop against a *trivially cheap* verifier — the rules of Go, a proof checker — and produce genuinely novel capability that no human designed and no human can verify move by move. AlphaGo's famous move 37 was "verified" only by eventually winning the game. There the loop is emphatically not a mere delivery mechanism, and the leverage lives in the search, not in a human. So the deflationary claim has to be stated more carefully than "the loop is the cheap part." The precise claim is this: **a loop can only manufacture capability that its verifier can completely score.** Go and formal proofs have oracles that are both cheap *and* total — they score the entire objective, which is exactly why search can run away from human comprehension safely. The trouble with most software is that its oracle is *partial*: tests exercise a fraction of intended behavior, and the properties that matter most in production — is this the right abstraction, will it be maintainable, did it actually do what the user meant — are precisely the ones no cheap verifier scores. A coding loop can therefore run away from human comprehension in the dimensions its tests cover and quietly drift in all the others. That is the difference between a loop that compounds into superhuman play and one that compounds into confident sludge: not the loop, but whether the oracle is complete.

This also answers the natural rejoinder that verifiers can co-evolve — that RLAIF, debate, LLM-as-judge, and generator/discriminator setups can *manufacture* cheaper, broader oracles rather than depending on a fixed human-authored one. They can, and this is the most important research direction against the thesis. But co-evolving the verifier does not dissolve the problem; it relocates it, because now you must trust the manufactured oracle, and an incomplete co-evolved oracle is exactly the failure the Darwin Gödel Machine's authors warn about below — a self-improvement loop that amplifies whatever its evaluation fails to capture. The oracle question is not escaped by making the oracle learned. It is made recursive.

The practitioners who actually run these loops overnight say this in almost identical words. Eric Carlson, after pointing a loop at a product for a week, concluded: ["the loop is not the interesting part. The dispatcher and the verification gate are."](https://amplitude.com/blog/ralph-loop) Blake Crosley's [overnight-loop failure catalogue](https://blakecrosley.com/blog/ralph-agent-architecture) includes the signature symptom: when the success criteria were trivial, the agent wrote degenerate `assert True` tests — it optimized the verifier out of existence rather than satisfy it. Rich Snapp, who built [his own deterministic orchestrator](https://www.richsnapp.com/article/2026/03-30-automating-your-agents) rather than let an LLM drive the control flow, found the sharpest version: LLM-driven loops "self-recover from problems in ways that look like they worked but were actually masking process issues." A loop with a weak signal doesn't fail loudly. It succeeds falsely.

This is why Ronacher is careful to say harness loops work well for [mechanically verifiable or disposable output](https://lucumr.pocoo.org/2026/6/23/the-coming-loop/) — porting, benchmarking, security scanning — and tend to produce code "too defensive, too complex, too local in its reasoning" for durable production systems. It is why Huntley's [porting recipe](https://ghuntley.com/porting/) works: the source program's own tests become the oracle, and citations back to the original source tease the agent into grounding itself. And it is why OpenAI's million-line result leaned on [enforced invariants, not implementations](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering) — architectural boundaries checked in code, "golden principles" enforced by automated cleanup agents. In every one of these software success stories, someone built the oracle first — and for production code, unlike for Go, building a *complete* oracle is the expensive part that does not go away.

The binding constraint on each rung, then, is not model capability. It is the **completeness and cost of the closing signal.** A smarter model inside a loop whose verifier scores only part of what matters just reaches a partly-wrong answer more persuasively.

## Loops that compound versus loops that merely repeat

If the signal is what matters, it draws a clean line between two kinds of outer loop that the hype tends to blur.

A loop **merely repeats** when it re-runs the agent and hopes — same prompt, fresh context, cross your fingers. This is the naive Ralph loop, and it works precisely and only where the output is cheaply verifiable or genuinely disposable. Point it at a migration with a full test suite and it shines. Point it at novel product design and it accumulates plausible sludge.

A loop **compounds** when each iteration improves the machinery that governs the next one — when a failure becomes a permanent constraint the loop can no longer violate. Osmani names this mechanism ["The Ratchet"](https://addyosmani.com/blog/agent-harness-engineering/): every agent failure becomes a rule encoded in the environment. Cursor's ["Scaling Long-Running Autonomous Coding"](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents) found that flat self-coordination among agents failed — they became risk-averse and duplicated work — and that a **Planner–Worker–Judge** hierarchy, with an explicit completion judge, was what let hundreds of agents run for weeks. The compounding is in the Judge and in the ratchet, not in the raw iteration count. Weng's self-harness and the Agentic Harness Engineering paper are the formal end of the same idea: the loop searches over its own scaffolding, keeping what measurably helps.

The distinction is decisive because compounding requires a *measurement*, and measurement requires a verifier. A repeating loop can get away with a weak oracle for a while (the errors are shallow and cheap to catch). A compounding loop cannot: it is optimizing against its verifier, so any blind spot in the oracle gets *amplified* generation over generation. Which brings us to the ceiling.

## The ceiling: the human does not parallelize

The reason none of this resolves into full autonomy is that the one resource every rung ultimately routes through — human judgment — does not scale with the loops.

Osmani's ["The Orchestration Tax is You"](https://addyosmani.com/blog/orchestration-tax/) is the sharpest statement: you are the **Global Interpreter Lock**. Agents run concurrently, but when any of their work needs genuine architectural understanding, it must acquire the lock, and there is exactly one lock. By Amdahl's Law, throughput equals the throughput of the review step; spawning more agents optimizes the part that was never the constraint. Christoph Nakazawa, running six agent-driven projects at once, [reports the same from the inside](https://cpojer.net/posts/modern-engineering-values): "I'm bottlenecked on exercising judgment." Steve Yegge's [**discernment horizon**](https://steve-yegge.medium.com/the-flat-curve-society-36c8b01eb33b) is the individual-scale version: beyond the point where you can verify a more powerful model's output, a smarter model is indistinguishable from a merely confident one, and additional capability becomes useless *to you*. Jacky Liang's ["Novelty Bottleneck"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27438) (March 2026) formalizes it: verification effort does not fall proportionally as AI capability rises, so once output novelty exceeds human verifiability, "the human becomes the limiting factor rather than AI capability itself." Osmani's [Agentic Autonomy Levels](https://addyosmani.com/blog/agentic-autonomy-levels/) turns this into planning advice — match autonomy to a task's risk and reversibility, because "verification will always be the bottleneck," and do not maximize it.

The empirical picture, so far as the young evidence allows, backs this up. METR's [task-completion time horizons](https://metr.org/time-horizons/) are widely quoted for the ~7-month doubling of the duration an agent can handle — but the headline is the *50%-reliability* horizon. The *80%*-reliability horizon is several times shorter — commonly cited at around 5x. Restated: the "day-long autonomous agent" headline describes roughly coin-flip reliability, not dependable work; hold the agent to a success rate you would actually deploy and the horizon collapses to something far shorter. The ["Long-Horizon Task Mirage"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11978) study (April 2026, 3,100+ trajectories) found success rates degrade *non-linearly* with horizon, with planning failures and memory/error-accumulation coming to dominate as the horizon grows — and, pointedly, that model differences wash out once failures dominate. Bigger models do not fix compounding error; they just push the cliff back a little. This is the empirical anchor the loop cluster has been arguing toward from the outside, and it is the mechanism Mario Zechner named as ["compounding booboos"](https://mariozechner.at/posts/2026-03-25-thoughts-on-slowing-the-fuck-down/): agents don't learn from mistakes within a run, and there is no natural bottleneck slowing the accumulation.

The multi-agent version is worse, not better. The MAST taxonomy ([Cemri et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657), 1,600+ traces) found most multi-agent failures are organizational and design failures, not capability failures — the fix is "better orchestration, not larger models." And ["The Illusion of Multi-Agent Advantage"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13003) (June 2026) found automatically-designed multi-agent systems *consistently underperform* plain chain-of-thought with self-consistency "despite being up to 10x more expensive," producing "architectural bloat that prioritizes superficial complexity." Orchestration doesn't emerge for free; it has to be paid for in human design effort — which is exactly the resource that doesn't parallelize. This is [Cognition's "Don't Build Multi-Agents"](https://cognition.ai/blog/dont-build-multi-agents) argument, now with a cost figure attached.

## The self-improvement mirage, specifically

The top rung deserves its own scrutiny, because "self-improving harness" is the phrase most likely to be oversold.

The honest literature is clear that scaffold self-improvement is real but bounded. STOP — the [Self-Taught Optimizer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02304) — is explicit that because the model's weights are never altered, "this is *not* full recursive self-improvement"; the gains ride entirely on a base model capable enough to write good scaffolding. Weng's own entry carries the load-bearing caution: GPT-3.5-class models *degraded* when allowed to optimize their own harness. The [Darwin Gödel Machine](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22954) shows genuine self-improvement — it raised its own score on SWE-bench from 20.0% to 50.0% (and on the Polyglot benchmark from 14.2% to 30.7%) — but its own limitations section is the skeptical payload: a single run takes about two weeks of heavy API spend, improvements can be benchmark-overfitting, and — the sentence that matters most — "if evaluation benchmarks do not fully capture all desired properties, the self-improvement loop could amplify misalignment over successive generations."

That last point is the whole argument in miniature. A self-improving loop is a compounding loop, and a compounding loop amplifies whatever its verifier fails to catch. Recursion does not escape the oracle problem; it multiplies the oracle's blind spots. The more autonomous the loop, the more totally its output is a function of the quality of the signal you gave it to optimize against.

## The uncomfortable implication

Put the rungs, the signal, and the ceiling together and the conclusion is almost inconvenient. The leverage in higher-level orchestration is not in the loop. It is in the verifier. "Loop engineering," done honestly, is mostly **verifier engineering**: building the tests, invariants, judges, benchmarks, and encoded taste that let a loop tell truth from confident fiction. That is precisely the work that cannot be handed to the loop, because it is the work of deciding what "correct" means — and a loop can only optimize against a definition of correct that has already been externalized into something executable, whether authored by a human or by an earlier loop whose own oracle you are then trusting on faith.

This reframes the whole stack. Böckeler's insight that a good harness should not eliminate human input but *route it to where it matters most* is the design principle. NK Schjødt's prescription — [move developers off code-review gatekeeping and onto building the enforcement systems](https://ai-blog.nkschjoedt.com/everyone-produces-code-now.html) that make review unnecessary — is the same move at the org level, though the essay's own ceiling argument bounds it: enforcement systems can retire review only in the regime where the oracle is complete enough to catch what review would have caught. For genuinely novel work, "making review unnecessary" is not an option, only a wish. Whoever builds the oracle owns the leverage; everyone else is feeding a loop whose failures they cannot see.

It is worth being honest that this is not a wholly new discovery. "The hard part is specifying what correct means, not producing candidate code" is Brooks's [essence-versus-accident](https://www.cgl.ucsf.edu/Outreach/pc204/NoSilverBullet.html) argument, and the test-first tradition, in modern dress. The loop era doesn't overturn that lesson; it raises the stakes on it, because a loop will act on an underspecified notion of "correct" thousands of times before a human looks. The contribution here is not the principle but its new sharpness: the faster and more autonomous the loop, the more completely the value of its output is determined by the completeness of a verifier written in advance.

## What would change this picture, and what the evidence doesn't yet support

Intellectual honesty requires marking the soft spots, including in the skeptical case.

The optimistic reading is not wrong where it applies. Where verification is cheap and mechanical — migrations with full test suites, performance work against a [real benchmark](https://github.com/Shopify/liquid/blob/2543fdc1a101f555db208fb0deeb2e3bf1ae9e36/auto/autoresearch.md), security scanning, porting — outer loops genuinely deliver, and the STOP/DGM results show scaffolds can be evolved rather than hand-tuned. This essay's thesis is not "loops don't work." It is "loops work exactly as far as their oracle reaches."

The evidence for the more expansive claims is thin, and the essay should say so plainly. The dramatic "shipped N repos overnight" and "102 features in a week" numbers are almost all self-reported, mostly greenfield, and mostly prototypes — Mathias Hansen, who ran the loops himself, draws the boundary explicitly: [greenfield side-projects yes, production no](https://www.geocod.io/code-and-coordinates/2026-01-27-ralph-loops), and warns that Ralph will "absolutely destroy your usage limits" (a colleague maxed two $400/mo subscriptions in days). It is also worth being explicit about provenance: several of the most flattering data points here — Cursor's "hundreds of agents for weeks," OpenAI's million lines, Amplitude's 102 features — are vendors describing the success of their own tools, and should be read at a lower evidentiary weight than the independent academic work (MAST, the long-horizon study, METR) they sit beside. The single-study empirical claims lean on throughout — Vella's 158-engineer survey, METR's reliability curves, the 2026 arXiv papers whose figures I have not personally re-derived — should each be read at the source before being leaned on, consistent with this collection's rule never to trust a number from a summary alone.

I should also state what would falsify the thesis rather than only defending it, since a claim that explains every outcome explains nothing. The thesis predicts: a loop turned loose against a *demonstrably incomplete* verifier should not produce durably correct output on novel, non-mechanical work. If someone exhibits a loop that reliably ships correct production software in a domain with no adequate test oracle, no human review, and no prior — durability confirmed by time, not by the loop's own say-so — the thesis is wrong, and the leverage really was in the loop after all. That is the observation to watch for. So far the durable successes cluster exactly where the theory predicts: complete, cheap oracles (games, proofs, tested migrations, benchmarked performance work).

The thing that would genuinely move the ceiling, then, is not a bigger model. It is *cheap, trustworthy, and complete verification of novel work* — a way to know an answer is right in a domain where you have no test, no benchmark, and no prior. Co-evolving verifiers are the field's bet that this can be manufactured; whether they can be trusted without a human re-checking them is the open question the whole top of the ladder rests on. Until it is answered, every new rung will keep doing the same thing: raising the altitude at which a human has to look, and quietly betting that the verifier below them is complete enough to hold.
